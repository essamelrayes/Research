Foundations of Science & Research

Start here: the vocabulary everything else builds on.

What is knowledge?

Implicit knowledge

Practical skills and hands-on know-how gained through experience.

Explicit knowledge

Theoretical understanding learnt through books or formal study.

The evolution of knowledge

  1. Curiosity

    The human pursuit to unravel the mysteries of the universe and the processes happening in nature.

  2. Observation

    Watching and noting natural phenomena. Before modern science, unexplained events were often attributed to supernatural powers.

  3. Trial & error

    Creating tools and devices by testing cause-and-effect relationships, including accidental discoveries and inventions.

  4. Systematic research

    A creative, accumulative, and systematic process designed to produce reliable knowledge.

Science and research

Science

A systematic and creative process that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of explanations and predictions about the universe.

Research

A set of logical, systematic, creative, and well-documented processes undertaken with a clear purpose — to build, organize, and expand knowledge.

Key definition. Research is a creative, accumulative, and systematic process in order to create reliable knowledge.

Basic vs. applied research

Basic (Pure)Applied
PurposeExpand theoretical understanding and produce universal principles of significance to society in general.Solve a specific, real-world problem; findings of direct value to managers and practice.
ContextUniversity-based; researcher-chosen topic and objectives; flexible timescales.Commissioned by organisations; tight timescales; directed toward immediate solutions.
"Research is to see what everybody else has seen and to think what nobody else has thought." — Albert Szent-Györgyi

Why we conduct research

Learning is endless and knowledge is limitless. Research builds a body of knowledge that is continually refined as beliefs are rejected or confirmed on the basis of testing empirical evidence.

Thomas Kuhn's Cycle

How scientific frameworks form, stabilise, encounter crisis, and revolutionise.

  1. Pre-science

    Research begins with broad exploration and observation, where no unified model guides inquiry. Different researchers may interpret phenomena using competing assumptions.

  2. Normal Science

    Researchers work within an accepted framework to develop and refine knowledge, focusing on solving smaller puzzles inside the established rules rather than questioning the foundational model itself.

  3. Model Drift & Crisis

    As inconsistencies and anomalies emerge that the existing model cannot satisfactorily explain, the validity of the current paradigm is tested — leading to model drift and potential scientific crisis.

  4. Model Revolution & Paradigm Change

    Once a new candidate model is validated and explains the anomalies, it replaces the old paradigm. This newly validated knowledge becomes the foundation for the next era of normal science.

The four knowledge-creation steps

Exploration→Establishment→Evaluation→Evolution

Pragmatism is the "third paradigm" — it values using all available tools to understand a research problem, rather than committing to one philosophical stance.

Reasoning & Qualitative Designs

How inference connects theory and empirical data, alongside the six core qualitative methodologies.

Three types of reasoning

Deductive

Theory → Data. Starts with an established theory or hypothesis and collects data to test it — moving from the general to the specific. Characteristic of Positivism and quantitative designs.

Inductive

Data → Theory. Begins with specific observations and patterns in empirical data to generate new concepts or theories — moving from the specific to the general. Characteristic of Interpretivism and qualitative research.

Abductive

Theory ↔ Data. The "logic of discovery." Begins with incomplete observations and forms the most likely explanation, moving iteratively between theory and data. Ideal for mixed methods.

Qualitative research designs

Ethnography
Immersive study of cultural groups, shared norms, language, and behaviours in natural settings over a sustained period. The researcher is typically an observer or participant observer.
Phenomenology
Study of phenomena as lived experiences — concerned with the perception of reality from the perspective of the individual.
Action Research
A collaborative, cyclical approach where researchers and participants work together to identify problems, implement solutions, and evaluate outcomes in cycles of improvement.
Case Study
In-depth, contextual investigation of a bounded system — a person, institution, organisation, or country — used to shed light on a phenomenon within its context.
Grounded Theory
Systematic inductive generation of an explanatory theory grounded directly in empirical data through constant comparison and theoretical sampling procedures.
Historical Research
Critical examination and interpretation of archival records and past events to understand change over time and learn what solutions have been used.

Mixed Methods Research

Combining quantitative breadth and qualitative depth for a more complete picture.

Definition. Mixed methods research uses quantitative (QUAN) and qualitative (QUAL) approaches across questions, methods, data collection, analysis, and inferences in a single study. It is not merely combining numbers and narratives — it is a methodological orientation associated with pragmatism.

Purposes

PurposeDescription
Exploring (Discovery)Generating refined hypotheses through initial qualitative exploration that informs subsequent instrument development and quantitative testing.
ExplainingIlluminating quantitative results with qualitative follow-up to uncover causal mechanisms behind statistical relationships.
Completing missingAchieving comprehensive understanding by integrating statistical patterns with rich narrative insights from participant interviews and open-ended responses.

Four core designs

Convergent Parallel Design
Quantitative and qualitative data are collected simultaneously, analysed separately, then merged to compare results. Used for triangulation — comparing different evidence types to enhance credibility and depth. Example: Likert-scale surveys alongside in-depth interviews on nurse job satisfaction.
Explanatory Sequential Design
Quantitative data are collected and analysed first; qualitative data follow second to explain or elaborate on the quantitative results. Example: A survey shows an ethnic group underperforms academically; follow-up interviews explore cultural, social, or institutional factors.
Exploratory Sequential Design
Qualitative data are collected and analysed first to explore an under-studied phenomenon; quantitative testing follows second to measure prevalence or generalise findings. Example: Interviews about community perceptions of climate change inform a questionnaire for a larger population.
Embedded Design
One type of data (qualitative or quantitative) is embedded within a broader framework guided predominantly by the other. The embedded method plays a secondary, supportive role. Example: A large quantitative study of a smoking-cessation programme embeds qualitative interviews with a small subgroup.

Key challenges

ChallengeWhat it involves
Integration difficultiesMeaningfully combining qualitative and quantitative data — often resulting in parallel rather than integrated analyses ("data silos").
Resource intensivenessRequires substantial time, funding, and expertise across multiple methodological traditions.
Paradigmatic tensionsReconciling positivism and interpretivism with fundamentally different ontological and epistemological assumptions.
Divergent or conflicting resultsWhen findings contradict, researchers need interpretive frameworks to address discrepancies rather than overemphasising one set of results.

Mitigating the challenges

  • Enhanced education — graduate-level training in integration techniques and philosophical foundations.
  • Robust planning — detailed integration protocols before data collection begins.
  • Collaborative teams — complementary methodological expertise across traditions.

Self-test

Answer in your head first, then open the card.

What is the difference between implicit and explicit knowledge?
Implicit knowledge is practical, experiential know-how — skills applied in action. Explicit knowledge is codified, theoretical understanding learnt through books or formal study.
What are the four stages of Kuhn's cycle?
Pre-science → Normal Science → Model Drift & Crisis → Model Revolution & Paradigm Change.
Which paradigm pairs with deductive reasoning, and which with inductive?
Positivism pairs with deductive reasoning (theory → data; general to specific). Interpretivism pairs with inductive reasoning (data → theory; specific to general).
What is abductive reasoning, and why does it suit mixed methods?
Abduction is the "logic of discovery" — it moves iteratively between theory and data, forming the most likely explanation from incomplete observations. It suits mixed methods because it stays open to surprising data and lets theories be revised rather than rigidly confirmed.
Name the four core mixed methods designs.
Convergent Parallel, Explanatory Sequential, Exploratory Sequential, and Embedded Design.
What is the main purpose of a Convergent Parallel Design?
Data triangulation — collecting quantitative and qualitative data simultaneously, analysing them separately, and merging results to see whether they confirm or complement each other.
What causes paradigmatic tensions in mixed methods research?
Reconciling positivism (singular objective reality, researcher independence) with interpretivism (multiple constructed realities, researcher interaction) — fundamentally different ontological and epistemological assumptions.
What is the difference between basic and applied research?
Basic (pure) research expands fundamental theoretical understanding and produces universal principles. Applied research uses theories and methods to solve specific, real-world problems with direct value to managers and practice.
